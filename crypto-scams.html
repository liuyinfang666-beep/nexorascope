CRYPTO SCAM PREVENTION

Crypto Scam Prevention: Warning Signs to Know

Recognize common crypto scams before you act. Learn how phishing, impersonation, fake investment offers, malicious approvals, and recovery scams work.

Independent educational guide · Last reviewed November 17, 2026

In this guide: Phishing · Fake support · Investment scams · Malicious approvals · Recovery scams · What to do

1. Phishing sites, emails, and messages

Phishing messages imitate wallet providers, exchanges, projects, government agencies, or people you know. They may claim an account is suspended, a wallet needs to be synchronized, a withdrawal is blocked, or urgent action is required. The goal may be to steal passwords, capture a one-time code, install malware, or trick you into signing a transaction.

  • Check the full domain carefully, including spelling and subdomains.
  • Do not use unexpected links or phone numbers in urgent messages. Navigate through a bookmark or a known official website.
  • Be suspicious of attachments, QR codes, and requests to install wallet updates from unofficial sources.
  • Never reveal passwords, recovery phrases, private keys, or authentication codes to someone who contacts you unexpectedly.

2. Fake customer support and impersonation

Scammers may monitor public posts and reply with fake support accounts, or send direct messages pretending to represent a wallet, exchange, token project, or public figure. They may offer to “validate” a wallet, fix a failed transaction, unlock funds, or help with a withdrawal.

Find support from the provider’s official website or app, not from a profile that approaches you first. A genuine support process should not ask you to disclose your recovery phrase or private key, install remote-access software, or send crypto to a personal address to prove ownership.

3. Fake investment opportunities and guaranteed returns

Fraudulent schemes may promise guaranteed profits, unusually high returns, exclusive access, or risk-free arbitrage. They may show fabricated dashboards or allow a small withdrawal to build trust before asking for a larger deposit, tax, fee, or “verification” payment.

Be cautious of unsolicited investment offers, pressure to act quickly, secret strategies, referral-driven returns, and requests to transfer funds to an address controlled by someone else. Verify the people, company, registration claims, and product independently. No screenshot or displayed account balance proves that funds can be withdrawn.

4. Giveaway, airdrop, job, and romance-related scams

Fake giveaways often claim that sending crypto will result in a larger amount being returned. Fake airdrops may lead to malicious sites that request wallet connections, approvals, or signatures. Fraudulent job offers may require an upfront crypto payment or ask you to move funds on behalf of an unknown person. Romance and friendship scams can build trust over time before introducing a supposedly exclusive crypto investment.

Do not send crypto to receive a promised giveaway. Do not pay an upfront fee to get a job. Treat investment advice from a new online contact with caution, even if the relationship has developed over weeks or months.

5. Malicious transaction prompts and token approvals

A deceptive website can ask you to sign a transaction or grant a token or NFT permission that is broader than you expect. A wallet connection alone is not the same as a token approval, but signing an approval can create an on-chain permission. Read the wallet prompt and verify the application, network, contract, recipient or spender, and permission scope.

If a prompt is unreadable or unexpected, stop. Do not sign merely to see a reward, verify an account, or cancel a fake incoming transfer. See Understanding Token Approvals.

6. Recovery scams after a loss

After someone loses crypto, another scammer may claim to be a recovery specialist, investigator, lawyer, exchange employee, or government official. They may promise guaranteed recovery in exchange for an upfront fee, tax, wallet connection, or account details. Treat unsolicited recovery offers with extreme caution. Never pay a stranger to release or return funds, and never provide wallet secrets.

The FTC explains common recovery-scam tactics in its guidance: Don’t pay money for help recovering money.

7. What to do if you think you interacted with a scam

  1. Stop. Do not click further links, sign additional prompts, or send more funds to the same person or service.
  2. Protect accounts. From a trusted device, secure affected email and exchange accounts, change compromised passwords, review active sessions, and enable MFA or passkeys where supported.
  3. Assess wallet exposure. If a recovery phrase or private key may have been exposed, treat the associated wallet as potentially compromised. Use official wallet guidance and consider professional security assistance before moving remaining assets.
  4. Review permissions. If you signed an approval, review the relevant network and spender using a trusted method. Understand the transaction before attempting to reduce or revoke a permission.
  5. Preserve evidence. Save transaction hashes, wallet addresses, URLs, usernames, messages, and timestamps. Do not publish secret credentials.
  6. Report through official channels. Contact the relevant exchange, wallet provider, platform, or local consumer-protection/law-enforcement authority using independently verified contact details.

Be aware that blockchain transfers may be difficult or impossible to reverse. Avoid anyone who guarantees recovery or demands payment in crypto to release funds.

Frequently asked questions

Will a legitimate wallet support agent ask for my recovery phrase?

No. Do not share a recovery phrase or private key with support staff, websites, or anyone who contacts you. Use official support channels and keep secret credentials private.

Is a wallet connection the same as approving a token?

No. A connection can let a website see a public wallet address or request actions, while a token approval is a separate on-chain permission. Always read the wallet prompt before signing.

Can crypto transactions be reversed after a scam?

Many blockchain transfers are difficult or impossible to reverse once confirmed. Contact the relevant platform and authorities promptly, preserve evidence, and be wary of people promising guaranteed recovery.

Should I pay a recovery service that contacted me first?

Be extremely cautious. Unsolicited recovery offers that demand upfront fees, crypto payments, or wallet credentials are common follow-up scams. Verify any service independently and never disclose wallet secrets.

What is the first thing to do after clicking a suspicious link?

Stop interacting, do not sign further prompts or enter credentials, and assess whether you entered a password, installed software, or approved a transaction. Secure affected accounts from a trusted device and seek official guidance.
